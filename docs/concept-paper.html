Do Automated Tutoring-Quality Metrics Predict Real Physics Learning?

A concept paper and preregisterable research prospectus for the validity-bridge study — and the case that this is the measurement question the entire field of LLM tutoring has skipped.

Author: chaoticcuriosity-io  ·  Status: concept paper / prospectus for Physics Education Research collaborators & funders  ·  Builds on: v1 · v2 · v3 plan  ·  Evidence base: primary-source-verified (see references & corpus)
The argument in one paragraph

AI tutors are being deployed to millions of students, and a research literature has grown up to evaluate them — but it evaluates process. Every published LLM-tutoring benchmark scores how a tutor behaves (does it scaffold, withhold answers, diagnose misconceptions) and none has shown that those scores predict how much a real student actually learns7–13. Meanwhile the field has begun training and ranking tutors on simulated-student learning proxies that have never been checked against real gains4,5. This is a validity gap at the foundation of a fast-moving field. We propose the experiment that closes it: a preregisterable randomized study that drives real introductory-physics learners across a wide gradient of LLM tutors, measures conceptual learning with a validated inventory under modern psychometrics, and tests whether the cheap automated tutoring-quality scores the field computes actually predict that learning. The headline within-study bridge is well-powered; the stronger "replace real students with a simulated pipeline" bridge is honestly underpowered at a single site and is seeded here for multi-site accumulation. The design is disconfirmable and publishable either way: if the proxy is valid, automated evaluation is licensed and the field can scale responsibly; if it is not, a foundational result redirects everyone — us included — away from a metric of unknown worth. This document specifies that study to a standard built to survive a hostile measurement, statistics, ethics, and funding review, and situates it in the research program it anchors.

1. The gap, and why it is the field's keystone problem

Two facts about the current moment do not fit together. First, AI tutors now demonstrably move real learning: a randomized study at Harvard found a GPT-4 physics tutor outperformed in-class active learning at an effect size of d = 0.63 (a ceiling-attenuated linear estimate; quantile regression puts it at 0.73–1.3 SD), with students learning more in less time1; the Tutor CoPilot trial raised topic mastery by 4 percentage points across 900 tutors and 1,800 students from historically under-served schools2. Second, an entire benchmarking literature has emerged to tell us which tutors are good — and not one of its instruments has been validated against the learning those first studies measured.

MathDial7, CIMA8, the AI Teacher Test9, the Teacher–Student Chatroom Corpus10, Bridge11, and the current state of the art, MathTutorBench12, all score process or simulated proxies and carry no real-student learning criterion. Worse, the gap is now being papered over rather than closed: Scarlatos and colleagues train tutors with reinforcement learning against a simulated-student next-turn-correctness model and state plainly, "the most significant limitation of our work is that we do not experiment with real students"4; EducationQ defines an "Absolute Learning Gain" that is computed entirely over an LLM playing a student5. And off-the-shelf LLM judges, when scored against expert human ratings of tutoring quality, correlate negatively with humans on the harder pedagogical dimensions13. The field is optimizing, ranking, and in some cases training on metrics whose connection to learning is assumed, not demonstrated.

The empty cell. Real-learning RCTs of LLM tutors exist (they measure whether a deployed tutor teaches). Metric-bearing benchmarks exist (they score process, or a simulated outcome). No study has computed a cheap automated tutoring-quality score on a transcript and shown it predicts that same student's measured real learning gain. That link — automated evaluation metric → real learning — is the contribution. We are not re-measuring whether AI tutors teach; we are testing whether the cheap score the field uses to rank them is a valid stand-in for learning.

This is why the bridge is the keystone. Until it exists, every leaderboard number in LLM tutoring — including our own v1 — is uninterpretable as evidence about teaching, and any optimization against those numbers risks Goodhart's law at the scale of millions of learners. The deliverable of this study is the field's first answer to the question every benchmark has so far assumed.

2. Relation to prior art — surviving the expert reviewer

A concept this load-bearing must concede what is already known. Two bodies of work bound our novelty, and we position against each explicitly.

2.1 The intelligent-tutoring-systems canon already bridged process to learning

Thirty years of intelligent-tutoring-systems (ITS) research connected hand-engineered cognitive models to measured learning: Cognitive Tutors and knowledge-tracing systems were validated against achievement, and VanLehn's synthesis places ITS at d ≈ 0.76, nearly matching human tutoring's d ≈ 0.7932. We do not claim to be first to ask whether instructional process predicts learning. Our increment is categorical and previously untested: LLM-as-judge and reward-model metrics are cognitive-model-free, trained on process preferences rather than outcomes, and documented to carry position, verbosity, and self-preference biases49,50,51. Whether that class of instrument predicts learning is open — and is exactly what every 2023–2025 LLM-tutoring benchmark assumes without evidence. We therefore retire v1's overreach: a null result would refute this class of automated proxy under test, not "the entire paradigm."

2.2 The adjacent LLM-tutor RCTs measure the endpoint, not the link

The honest framing distinguishes individual-level bridging (ours) from the work closest to it:

StudyWhat it establishesWhy it is not the bridge

Its outcome was a researcher-made test, with the FCI only as a baseline covariate — itself an argument for our validated-inventory design.

So "first to test the link" is accurate, provided we state it precisely: first to estimate whether a cheap automated transcript score predicts an individual real student's measured conceptual gain. Tutor CoPilot is acknowledged as the nearest neighbor and distinguished on exactly the dimensions above, pre-empting the "why is this novel?" attack.

3. The validity framework — what the study can and cannot license

We frame the enterprise in the terms a measurement reviewer expects: Messick's unified validity36 and the Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing37. Validity is the warrant for a specific use of a score; here the intended use is selecting or ranking tutors for deployment.

  • What the bridge supplies. The within-study correlation of a process-score with a learner's gain is concurrent criterion evidence plus internal-structure evidence — not yet predictive or consequential validity. We upgrade toward predictive validity by estimating the bridge with leave-one-condition-out cross-validation, so the headline number reflects prediction on tutors not used to fit it38.
  • Consequential validity is a named strand, not an afterthought. A score valid on average but inverted for low-prior-knowledge learners is a consequential-validity failure even if the global gate passes — so equity enters the validity argument and the decision gate (§10), not just a subgroup table.
  • Construct-irrelevant variance is named on both sides. Student ability, dose, and tutor verbosity confound not only the gain but the bridge itself (§8.3) — the single most likely route to a falsely-positive result, and one prior framings missed.
  • The construct. Tutoring quality = eliciting durable conceptual change through generative student reasoning (ICAP-anchored35) while avoiding the installation of wrong physics — operationalized as the six behaviorally-anchored dimensions of the v2 rubric, whose dimensionality is treated as an empirical question, not an assumption (§8.4).

4. Research questions, estimands & hypotheses

Following the estimand discipline of ICH E9(R1)40, each question commits to a population, predictor/treatment, outcome, intercurrent-event handling, and summary, and is labelled causal or predictive. All hypotheses, models, exclusions, and gates are preregistered (filed OSF preregistration or a Registered Report) before data collection.

  • RQ1 — manipulation (causal). Do LLM tutors produce conceptual gains above a no-tutor control? Calibrated to d ≈ 0.4–0.8, anchored by VanLehn's tutoring ceiling32 and Kestin's single-site envelope1 — not the mythical "two-sigma."
  • RQ2 — the B1 bridge (predictive / criterion-validity). Does the transcript process-score predict an individual student's covariate-adjusted latent gain, within condition and within ability stratum? Labelled predictive, never causal.
  • RQ3 — the B2 bridge (predictive, accumulating). Does the fully-automated simulated-student pipeline score predict condition-level real gain? Powered as multi-site accumulation, not a single-site verdict.
  • RQ4 — discriminant ordering (a separate check). Do the anchored foils rank as expected on both tiers? This is a manipulation/known-ordering sanity check, held separate from the criterion-validity headline so the engineered spread cannot inflate it (§6.4).

One primary confirmatory bridge estimand is protected at a fixed α; per-dimension, per-topic, and subgroup analyses are exploratory with hierarchical partial pooling or FDR control44 — closing the "which dimension carries the prediction?" garden of forking paths47 that could otherwise manufacture a "valid" dimension by chance.

5. Measuring learning (Tier G) — the criterion, specified to PER standard

This is where a hostile measurement reviewer attacks, so it is specified, not gestured at.

5.1 Outcome: covariate-adjusted latent change, not raw normalized gain

Raw normalized gain ⟨g⟩14 is demoted from primary to a reported-for-comparability secondary. Its confounds are documented and load-bearing: individual ⟨g⟩ correlates with scientific-reasoning ability (Lawson) at r ≈ 0.51 — more strongly than with pre-score20 — so a primary outcome more correlated with a stable aptitude than with prior knowledge is a weak criterion for ranking tutors. The registered primary outcome is a Rasch/2PL latent-ability change score with concurrent pre/post calibration via common anchor items, analyzed as post θ ~ condition + pretest θ + Lawson reasoning in the crossed mixed model18. Raw ⟨g⟩ and Cohen's d are reported alongside for comparability only.

The g-vs-d debate is live, not settled — and we don't pretend otherwise. Nissen et al. (2018) show ⟨g⟩ is biased toward high-pretest populations and recommend Cohen's d21; Coletta & Steinert (2020) rebut that the bias is an omitted-variable artifact22; Nissen et al. (2024) re-assert and extend the critique23; Navarrete et al. (2024) argue the pretest correlation can be measurement-error, leaving ⟨g⟩ unbiased24. This three-sided debate is the reason we commit to covariate-adjusted latent change rather than trusting any single scalar.

5.2 Inventory choice and the subscale trap

The FCI15 and FMCE17 are not interchangeable — multidimensional IRT recovers ~8 reasoning principles in the FMCE versus ~19 in the FCI19 — so the inventory is chosen per misconception cluster, not treated as one knob. Critically, we do not assume FCI "cluster subscales" are scoreable: the FCI's factor structure is contested27. We use either the full-inventory latent score or a purpose-built, content-validated, reliability-gated item set assembled in Phase 0, with dimensionality established empirically (the Mechanics Baseline Test16 and item-response-curve diagnostics26 are available where a more quantitative or distractor-level outcome is wanted). Two-tier items28 capture the reasoning a single forced choice omits.

5.3 Reliability, equating, and the noise floor

Up to ~25% of inventory answers flip on retest with no learning25, so measurement noise is first-class. We report criterion reliability (IRT marginal reliability; parallel-form reliability) so the bridge can be disattenuated; make parallel-form equating an explicit Phase-0 acceptance gate (IRT linking via common anchors, reporting the equating residual30), acknowledging that no standard validated FCI parallel form exists; and measure Lawson reasoning at pre only, as a stable baseline covariate, to avoid adjusting away tutoring-induced reasoning gains.

6. The automated scores (Tier P) & the two bridges

6.1 The candidate proxies

The transcripts that real students actually experience are scored three ways in parallel: by (i) a human-expert rater on the v2 behaviorally-anchored, ICAP-grounded rubric; by (ii) a calibrated LLM judge; and by (iii) a trained domain reward model (in the lineage of MathTutorBench12). A separate fully-automated simulated-student pipeline produces a per-condition score with no real students — the cheap pipeline whose validity we ultimately want to certify.

6.2 The two bridges

BridgeEstimatorPowerWhat it licenses
B1 — rubric on real sessionsStudent transcript score ↔ that student's adjusted latent gain; crossed mixed-effects (random student/item; condition fixed), within-condition partial association (N = hundreds)Well-poweredThat the scoring instrument tracks learning within study — the primary, guaranteed deliverable.
B2 — automated pipeline vs realityCondition-level simulated-pipeline score ↔ condition-mean real gain (K data points)Underpowered at single-site KThe strong claim — that the no-real-students pipeline can replace real testing. Seeded for meta-analytic accumulation.

6.3 Licensing automation honestly

The decisive comparison is the three-way bridge: automation is licensed only if the LLM-judge or reward-model bridge tracks the human-expert bridge. A reward-model bridge that beats the human bridge is treated as a red flag for leakage/contamination, not a success. The judge's Phase-0 gate is split into reliability (Krippendorff α ≥ 0.80 for this use) and validity (a transcript-feature bias audit showing scores are uncorrelated with length and student-ability proxies; no same-family self-judging) — because agreeing with humans about process is not evidence that process predicts learning, the very reliability-≠-validity error our v1 teardown identified, now guarded one level up.

6.4 Range, foils, and circularity

The anchored foils (answer-dumper, confidently-wrong-physics, pseudo-Socratic, expert) are defined by the same rubric Tier P scores, so including them mechanically inflates the bridge and makes RQ4's ordering near-tautological. We therefore report the headline bridge on the real model×prompt conditions, excluding the foils (with a with-foils sensitivity arm and a range-restriction correction48), and use the foils only to establish range and as the separate discriminant check. The leaderboard-licensing claim rests on the realistic deployable-tutor range, where the actual selection problem lives.

7. Study design

Conditions (K ≈ 10–16): a quality gradient spanning bad→expert — the four anchored foils, several base LLMs (local and frontier) crossed with prompt regimes (terse / scaffolding-forced / unconstrained), a no-tutor control (matched-time text), and, if feasible, a human-tutor reference arm to calibrate the effect-size ceiling.

Participants & assignment: real introductory-physics learners, covariate-adaptive (minimization) assignment on pre-score and reasoning ability (preferred over fragile fine stratification across two continuous covariates at K≈12), with all balancing covariates entered in every model.

  consent → pretest (validated inventory + Lawson reasoning + covariates)
         → time-boxed tutoring on a small misconception-cluster set   [transcript logged]
         → immediate posttest (equated parallel form)
         → delayed posttest (~2–4 weeks, durability)  [see harm-protocol resolution, §12]

Worked instantiation (mechanics): 3–4 clusters (e.g., Newton's third law, motion-requires-force, gravitation); FCI/FMCE latent outcome with cluster-aligned purpose-built items where tight pre/post-to-tutored mapping is needed; ~30-min focused sessions; target ≈ 60–90 per arm → several hundred consented learners (single large course, multi-section, or screened online pool). The existing run_local_eval.py / scoring stack instantiates the Tier-P side; the new build is the Tier-G RCT apparatus and the Phase-0 calibration.

8. Analysis & decision gates

8.1 Model

Crossed mixed-effects with random effects for student and item; condition treated as fixed for the RQ1 manipulation contrast (with K≈12, a condition/tutor random effect is singular and anticonservative43). Small-cluster inference via Kenward–Roger / Satterthwaite degrees of freedom or a parametric bootstrap. A formal preregistered Statistical Analysis Plan accompanies the protocol.

8.2 Power by simulation, not formula

The illustrative closed-form figures (e.g., "r = 0.3 needs ~85 pairs") are replaced by a simulation-based power analysis under the actual crossed model — with the assumed ICC/design effect and covariate multiple-R — powering B1 for the partial, between-condition-aware estimand and for the disattenuated gate (a hard M1 pilot deliverable with released simulation code42). Equivalence/non-inferiority framing (TOST41) makes a null a confident null rather than a non-significant estimate.

8.3 The co-production confound — blocked by design

The chief threat to a falsely-positive result: an abler, more-engaged student elicits richer tutor moves and learns more, manufacturing a spurious process-score↔gain link that licenses nothing about ranking tutors. We enter pre-score, Lawson reasoning, and dose (turns, student tokens, on-task time, transcript length) as covariates before the process-score; report the within-condition partial association and, separately, the between-condition slope (Mundlak / group-mean centering); add a shuffled-pairing placebo (a score on mismatched transcripts must not predict gain); and quantify robustness to unmeasured student-ability confounding with an E-value45. B1 is stated as predictive, never causal.

8.4 Dimensionality

Given v1's near-collinear dimensions, the confirmatory bridge uses a theory-driven ICAP-anchored composite or latent factor rather than six correlated slopes; per-dimension importance is exploratory with shrinkage.

GATE G1 Go/no-go (preregistered), restated to be defensible: the proxy is declared usable only if the lower bound of the disattenuated, cross-validated bridge CI clears a pre-set decision-relevant floor — correcting for the reliability of both the gain criterion and the process-score39, with the achievable ceiling computed in the power analysis, not assumed. An explicit equivalence branch separates "refuted" from "inconclusive–underpowered." This removes the original design's fatal flaw: a single point r ≥ 0.5 against a noisy criterion and an imperfect judge could be mechanically unreachable, turning a null into an attenuation artifact rather than a real finding.

Failure branch (de-risks the whole award): if the judge cannot be calibrated to track the human bridge, the human-scored B1 RCT still yields a guaranteed, publishable deliverable (does any tutoring score, even an expert human's, predict learning?). The simulated student is thus validated by the RCT, never assumed as a precondition for it.

Contamination controls are citable, not aspirational. Concept-inventory leakage into pretraining is a live, acknowledged risk56; canaries alone are insufficient (a frontier model reproduced the BIG-bench canary GUID57) and n-gram decontamination is defeated by paraphrase58. We pair a tiered-release item bank (open rubric + embargoed, credentialed-access live items with rotating canaries) with provable black-box auditing — exchangeability testing59 and Min-K% membership inference60 — and extend the audit to the judge and reward model inputs, not just the student-facing instrument61,62.

10. Broader impacts & equity

Equity is a first-class measurement issue, because normalized gain confounds with incoming ability and reasoning at both the student level (Lawson r ≈ 0.51) and the class-average level (pre-score r = 0.63)20 — so differential validity across prior-attainment and reasoning strata is the expected case, not an edge case. We therefore make it a powered hypothesis, not a subgroup footnote: do weak/foil tutors disproportionately harm low-prior-knowledge or lower-reasoning learners, and does the process-score↔gain bridge hold or invert within strata? A bridge valid on average but inverted for under-served learners is a consequential-validity failure (Messick) even if G1 passes globally — and the licensed use is then restricted to the strata where it holds.

Named beneficiaries: introductory-physics instructors at under-resourced, two-year, and minority-serving institutions, where unvalidated LLM tutors are most likely to be deployed at scale without learning evidence. The deliverable is a cheap, validated "is this tutor actually teaching?" instrument plus an open, contamination-controlled item bank.

External funder validation. The Gates-Foundation-backed Digital Promise "EDU AI" program (a K-12 AI Infrastructure RFP, up to ~$8M) independently frames our exact construct in funder-authored language: AI tutors "give answers too quickly," "talk too much," "do not always create enough space for students to work through problems," and exhibit a "Helpful Assistant Bias" that "gives away answers rather than supporting the student's learning process"68. The pedagogical construct PhysTutorBench measures is a currently-funded field priority (their RFP is K-12 math; the construct transfers cleanly to intro physics).

Funding-environment honesty. Both obvious federal homes face acute 2025–26 disruption. IES had ~$881–900M in research contracts cancelled and lost 100+ staff in early 2025 (verified)67. For NSF, a House FY2026 bill proposed cutting the agency ~23% to $7.0B and folding the STEM Education directorate into the general research line — but this was not enacted: a Continuing Resolution funded NSF at the FY2025 level (~$9.1B) with the directorate preserved71. We present the cut as a serious proposed-cut risk that argues for phasing and the negative-result value proposition — not as enacted fact.

12. Ethics, data & reproducibility

  • IRB & the wrong-physics harm protocol. Some learners are exposed to foil tutors that teach incorrect physics. We cap foil-exposure dose quantitatively, run a real-time guardrail that catches only safety-critical errors (so it does not contaminate the studied condition), and deliver corrective remediation.
  • The remediation-vs-durability conflict, resolved. Remediation is an instructional intervention; if delivered before the delayed posttest it would mean the delayed test measures remediation, not durability. We therefore deliver remediation immediately after the immediate posttest only for safety-critical content, administer uniform remediation to all arms so it is a constant rather than a condition-correlated dose, and pre-register handling of intervening course instruction in course-embedded designs.
  • Trial integrity. CONSORT flow, intention-to-treat primary analysis, attrition-by-arm reporting, and a stated missing-data model (multiple imputation / FIML under MAR with an MNAR sensitivity analysis)46, with N inflated for the differential foil-arm attrition the harm protocol invites.
  • Reproducibility. Versioned models/prompts/seeds; preregistration; open release of rubric, analysis and simulation code, consented transcripts, and the tiered item bank (held-out splits + rotating canaries). FCI access/copyright is handled through the credentialed-access tier.

The payoff. Whichever way Gate G1 resolves, the field gains its first evidence-based answer to the question every LLM-tutoring benchmark has assumed: do these scores mean anything about learning? A "yes" licenses cheap automated evaluation and responsible scale; a "no" is a foundational negative result that redirects the field — and protects students from optimization against a metric of unknown worth. That answer is the anchor the rest of the research program (v2 §6) is built on.
