PhysTutorBench v2: A PER-Grounded Methodology for Evaluating LLM Physics Tutors

A validity teardown of our v1 benchmark, an annotated literature corpus, a tiered (gold-standard + practical) methodology, and an instantiable study kit — designed as a jumping-off point for Physics Education Research groups studying or deploying LLMs in tutoring.

Author: chaoticcuriosity-io  ·  Status: design document / position paper (draft)  ·  Supersedes: PhysTutorBench-Local v1
Orientation

Our v1 study scored four local LLMs on the process quality of their physics tutoring, as judged by another LLM. It was internally informative but, by the standards of educational measurement, it measures the wrong thing in the wrong way: it never observes learning, its judge was never validated against humans, and its "student" was an LLM that can leak the answer. This document does three things. First (§1) it dismantles v1 against Messick's unified validity framework11 and the Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing12. Second (§2) it assembles the grounding literature, distinguishing claims we verified against primary sources from those that are canonical. Third (§3–§4) it specifies a superseding, tiered methodology — a gold-standard learning-gain study and a cheaper LLM-based tier whose validity is bridged to the gold tier — plus a ready-to-use item/rubric/preregistration kit. The through-line is one shift, forced by the evidence: stop rewarding judged tutoring moves and start measuring student learning, then earn the right to use cheaper proxies by calibrating them against that ground truth.

Provenance & method

1. Critical teardown of v1

Messick11 reframed validity as a single argument about whether the inferences we draw from scores are warranted, with facets that the Standards12 codify: evidence based on content, internal structure, relations to other variables (criterion), response processes, and consequences. v1 is weak on nearly all of them. The table maps each v1 design choice to the facet it threatens and the standard it violates.

v1 design choiceValidity / reliability threatWhy (with evidence)
Outcome = LLM-judged process quality (6 rubric dimensions), never any measured learningCriterion & consequential validity (the core failure)The thing we care about is whether students learn. v1 never observes a learner, so it has no criterion evidence. Worse, the field has repeatedly found that assumed-good process features don't track learning: VanLehn8 found the belief that finer interaction granularity raises effectiveness was not confirmed, and Chi et al.9 found students learned just as well when tutors were suppressed from giving explanations/feedback. Rewarding "good-looking" tutor moves can therefore be invalid.
"Transfer success" scored from the simulated student's answerConstruct validity — construct-irrelevant varianceThe simulated student is an LLM that already knows the physics; its transfer answer reflects its latent knowledge (and prompt compliance), not learning caused by the tutor. We observed exactly this leakage in v1. The measured score is contaminated by an irrelevant construct (the student model's competence).

LLM judges carry documented systematic biases — verbosity, position, and self-preference13 — that consistent re-scoring will not reveal. Without agreement against expert humans there is no criterion evidence the judge measures tutoring quality at all.

An LLM prompted to "hold a misconception" is not shown to backslide, partially restructure, or err like real students do; family overlap with a tutor invites rapport/style confounds.

Same-family authoring + judging is a closed loop that can reward a house style rather than pedagogy, and risks train/eval contamination.

LLM outputs are stochastic; a single run gives no run-to-run interval.

Bottom line. v1 is a reliability demonstration wearing a validity costume. Its single defensible quantitative claim — that one LLM judge applies a rubric to itself consistently (κ≈0.73) — says nothing about whether high-scoring tutors actually teach physics. Every fix below is tied to one of these rows.

2. Annotated literature corpus

Tags: verified = claim adversarially checked against the primary source in our research run (quote on file); canonical = foundational work cited from the corpus but not independently vote-verified here; contested = genuine disagreement in the literature. Full citations in References.

2a. PER measurement: gains, inventories, and their limits

  • verified Normalized gain ⟨g⟩ (Hake1) is the PER workhorse for course effectiveness: g = (post − pre)/(100 − pre). Across 62 courses (N=6542), interactive-engagement courses averaged ⟨g⟩=0.48 vs 0.23 for traditional — the canonical demonstration that how you teach moves conceptual learning about twofold. This is the outcome metric v2 adopts as ground truth.
  • verified The FCI (Hestenes, Wells & Swackhamer2) is a validated diagnostic of force/motion conceptions — an appropriate, defensible source of misconceptions and pre/post items (as v1 used), provided items are used as designed.
  • contested Is ⟨g⟩ the right metric? Nissen et al.4 argued normalized gain is prescore-biased and recommended Cohen's d; Coletta & Steinert3 rebutted that the bias is an omitted-variable artifact and that d is less informative as a single-parameter effectiveness measure. Implication: report both, and model student-level covariates rather than trusting any single scalar.
  • verified ⟨g⟩ is not population-independent. Coletta & Phillips7 found individual g correlates with pre-score (r≈0.15–0.33) and, more strongly, with scientific-reasoning ability (Lawson r≈0.51). Implication: incoming knowledge and reasoning ability are confounds that must be measured and adjusted for when comparing tutors across student groups.
  • verified Item-correctness ≠ understanding. On FCI Q.29, many students pick the right answer without the concept and many who learned it still miss it5; some items even get worse post-instruction. Implication: never score a single forced-choice answer as "transfer"; use multi-item subscales and, ideally, reasoning evidence.
  • verified Pre/post change is partly noise. Because responses are non-deterministic, up to ~25% of answers can flip on retest with no learning, and apparent gains/losses can range 0–100%6; IRT models these gains/losses better than raw proportions. Implication: power studies for measurement noise; prefer IRT/latent-trait scoring over raw % for individual-level inference.

2b. Tutoring & learning science: what "good tutoring" actually is

  • verified Realistic effect-size ceiling. VanLehn8: human tutoring vs no-tutoring is d≈0.79 — not Bloom's mythical "2 sigma" — and intelligent tutoring systems reach d≈0.76, nearly equal to humans. Implication: (i) LLM tutors are worth rigorous learning-outcome study; (ii) calibrate expectations and power to d≈0.4–0.8, not heroic gains.
  • verified Tutor talk is not the active ingredient. Chi et al.9 posed three hypotheses (tutor-centered, student-centered, interactive) and showed students learned just as well when tutors were suppressed from explaining/giving feedback — learning tracked student construction and scaffolding density. Implication: the construct "good tutoring" should center on eliciting student generative activity, which validates v1's instinct to reward answer-withholding — but it must be tied to learning, not assumed.
  • verified ICAP (Chi & Wylie 201410) predicts a monotonic ordering Passive < Active < Constructive < Interactive, operationalized via students' overt behaviors/outputs — a theory-grounded scaffold for a tutoring rubric (reward moves that push students to generate). contested the rigid ordering and the overt-behavior operationalization are challenged by Schwartz et al. 202317; use ICAP as a definitional scaffold, not a settled empirical law.
  • canonical Productive failure (Kapur14): having learners struggle/generate before instruction can beat direct instruction — a caution against over-rewarding immediate tutor "help." canonical Feedback (Hattie & Timperley; Black & Wiliam) and the self-explanation effect further support generative, formative designs.

2c. Validity & reliability methodology

  • canonical Messick's unified validity11 and the AERA/APA/NCME Standards12 are the backbone of §1 and §3: validity is an argument with content/structure/criterion/response-process/consequence evidence; the Educational Measurement (5th ed.) validity chapter16 is the working reference.
  • canonical Reliability for rubric scoring: Cohen's/Fleiss κ and quadratic-weighted κ for ordinal agreement, Krippendorff's α for ≥2 raters with missing data, and Generalizability Theory to decompose variance across rater/scenario/occasion facets — the right tool for a benchmark with crossed facets (v1 used none).
  • canonical Content validity by process: expert-panel review and cognitive interviews / think-aloud protocols are how PER instruments earn content and response-process validity — the antidote to v1's model-authored items.

2d. LLMs in education & evaluation

  • verified LLM-judge biases are documented and quantified. Zheng et al.13 name position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases plus limited reasoning, and set the bar: a strong judge (GPT-4) reaches >80% agreement with human preferences — but only on non-tie votes (including ties it falls to ~60–66%). Ye et al. (CALM)18 quantify 12 distinct judge biases and show position-bias robustness drops below 0.5 with 3–4 candidates; self-preference is independently confirmed (Panickssery et al.19, though its mechanism — self-recognition vs. perplexity/familiarity — is contested). Implication: a judge is not reliable by default — calibrate against expert humans, audit biases (randomize order, control length, never self-judge), and report agreement before any at-scale use.
  • verified Every prior LLM-tutoring benchmark scores process, not learning. MathDial20 (≈3k teacher–LLM-student dialogues with a teacher-move taxonomy; students are LLM-simulated, so a real gain is impossible by construction — and a 2025 follow-up finds simulated-student results inflated), CIMA21 (next-tutor-utterance generation scored by BLEU/BERTScore), the AI Teacher Test22 (comparative human judgment — both agents scored worse than human teachers, especially on helpfulness: fluency ≠ pedagogy), TSCC23 (dialogue corpus, SETT annotation), Bridge24 (expert remediation decisions, human preference; authors list learning impact as future work), and even the SOTA MathTutorBench25 (a trained reward model discriminating expert vs. novice at ≈0.84 accuracy) — none carries a real learning-outcome criterion. This is precisely the gap v2 fills, and it confirms v1 was not an outlier but the field norm. [Directly motivates §3.2.]
  • canonical A domain-calibrated reward model (as in MathTutorBench25) outperforms a generic LLM judge on pedagogical preference — a concrete judge architecture for §3.6 — though reward-model scores are trained on process preferences and still do not guarantee learning.
  • open here Real-world RCT & contamination were not independently verified in our research runs. A Tutor CoPilot–style RCT15 with real tutors/students is the kind of criterion design v2's gold tier emulates (cited, unverified here). Contamination/leakage of partly-web-available PER inventories is a real risk requiring held-out/novel items, canaries, and paraphrase robustness — treated here as a design requirement (§3.4, §3.7) pending a dedicated pinned source.

3. New methodology (tiered)

The design is a ladder. Tier G (gold) measures real student learning and is the criterion everything else must answer to. Tier P (practical) is the cheap, automatable LLM-based evaluation — usable only to the extent it has been shown to predict Tier G. The novel methodological commitment is the explicit validity bridge between them.

3.1 Construct definition

Operationalize "good physics tutoring," from §2b, as: maximizing a learner's durable conceptual gain by eliciting and scaffolding their own generative reasoning, diagnosing the specific prior conception, and avoiding the introduction or reinforcement of incorrect physics. Note what this excludes: fluent explanation and answer delivery are not the construct (Chi9), and process polish is valued only insofar as it produces learning (VanLehn8). [Fixes v1 rows 1, 2, 7.]

3.2 Outcome measure & the validity bridge

  • Tier G criterion: change on a validated concept inventory administered pre/post a tutoring intervention with real learners, randomized to tutor condition(s). Report normalized gain ⟨g⟩1 and Cohen's d3,4, with IRT/latent scoring6; model student-level covariates (pre-score, reasoning ability) given their confounding role7. Use delayed post-tests for durability and multi-item subscales rather than single items5.
  • Tier P signals: the automatable measures (rubric-scored process; simulated-student "learning"; judge ratings).
  • The bridge (the core contribution): do not assume Tier P predicts Tier G — estimate it. On a shared scenario set evaluated under both tiers, report the correlation/regression of Tier-P scores against Tier-G gains, with a pre-set minimum (e.g., criterion validity r ≥ 0.5 before Tier P may be used for ranking). Re-validate the bridge whenever the tutor population, judge model, or items change. [Fixes v1 rows 1, 2.]
Honest caveat (see §5): whether any current automated proxy reaches a usable bridge correlation with measured physics learning is an open empirical question. v2's contribution is making that question answerable, not assuming the answer.

3.3 Student modeling

Three options, in descending validity: (i) real learners (Tier G); (ii) human-validated simulated students — LLM students accepted only after their dialogue behavior is shown to match real student corpora (turn-level error/backslide rates, misconception persistence, response processes per the Standards12); (iii) naive LLM students (v1's choice) — usable for cheap iteration but not for validity claims. Mandate: the student model family must be disjoint from every tutor under test, and the simulated student must be prevented from accessing the canonical answer (no answer-leakage on transfer). [Fixes v1 rows 2, 4.]

3.4 Items / scenario sourcing & content validity

Source misconceptions from validated inventories (FCI/FMCE/CSEM/DIRECT/TCS/…) as v1 did, but earn content validity the PER way: an expert panel (physicists + PER specialists) authors/reviews scenarios; cognitive interviews with real students confirm each scenario elicits the intended conception. Never let the tutor's or judge's model family author the items. Maintain a held-out, contamination-controlled set (novel contexts, paraphrases, canaries). Sample across difficulty and prevalence, not just high-prevalence items. [Fixes v1 rows 5, 6.]

3.5 Rubric & its validation

Replace arbitrary dimensions/weights with a behaviorally-anchored rating scale (BARS) grounded in ICAP10 and the construct (§3.1): each level has observable descriptors and transcript exemplars. Validate the rubric: pilot it, run expert raters, report inter-rater reliability, and — crucially — derive dimension weights empirically from each dimension's relation to the Tier-G learning criterion (or report dimensions separately and refuse to composite until weights are earned). [Fixes v1 rows 1, 7.]

3.6 Judging & reliability

  • Humans are the criterion. Expert raters score a calibration sample; target weighted κ / Krippendorff α with pre-registered thresholds (e.g., α ≥ 0.67 acceptable, ≥ 0.80 good), using G-theory to size rater/scenario facets.
  • LLM judges only after calibration. An LLM judge may be used at scale only once its agreement with the human panel is measured and adequate, and after a bias audit: randomize transcript/option order (position bias), control/normalize length (verbosity bias), and forbid a model from judging its own family (self-enhancement)13. Concretely: strong general judges have matched human agreement (>80%) only under favorable conditions, and their robustness collapses (below 0.5) once there are more than two candidates18. A domain-calibrated reward model trained on expert pedagogical preferences (cf. MathTutorBench25) is a stronger default than a generic judge — but it must still be validated against the Tier-G learning criterion, not trusted on its training signal alone.
  • Panels & blinding: ≥3 independent judges, blind to tutor identity, randomized presentation; report agreement and disagreement analysis (not just a single test–retest κ as v1 did). [Fixes v1 rows 3, 5.]

3.7 Contamination, fairness & ethics

Contamination controls (held-out/novel items, canaries, paraphrase robustness) per §3.4. Equity: because gain confounds with incoming ability/reasoning7, pre-register subgroup analyses and report differential effectiveness; treat consequential validity (who benefits, over-reliance, accessibility) as part of the argument, not an afterthought. Ethics: Tier G requires IRB/consent, data governance, and a defined protocol for handling a tutor that teaches wrong physics to a real student. [Fixes v1 row 8.]

3.8 Statistics & power

Unit of analysis is the student (Tier G) or the (tutor × scenario × run) cell (Tier P). Use mixed-effects models with crossed random effects (student, scenario, judge, run) — not a flat mean of 48 numbers. Power to the realistic d≈0.4–0.8 ceiling8 and to measurement noise6. Estimate run-to-run variance by repeating generations at the intended temperature. Pre-register hypotheses and correct for multiple comparisons across dimensions/topics. [Fixes v1 rows 4, 6, 7.]

3.9 Model-agnostic core + local/open-weight recipe

The core (construct, criterion, bridge, rubric, reliability, contamination, ethics) is model-agnostic. A concrete local recipe: serve tutors via an OpenAI-compatible endpoint (e.g., Ollama, as in v1); for Tier P, use a disjoint local model as a human-validated simulated student and a separately-calibrated judge (local or API) whose human-agreement is measured first; keep all weights/prompts/seeds versioned for reproducibility. Local-first buys privacy, cost, and classroom deployability — but the validity bridge is what makes its numbers mean anything.

4.2 Behaviorally-anchored rubric (excerpt, one dimension)

LvlEliciting Generative Reasoning (ICAP-anchored)
0Tutor states the answer/explanation; student only receives (Passive).
1Tutor asks rhetorical/leading questions whose answer is given (Active in form, Passive in substance).
2Tutor prompts the student to apply a stated rule to the case (Active).
3Tutor elicits student-generated reasoning beyond given material — self-explanation, prediction, contrast (Constructive).
4Tutor sustains co-construction: builds on the student's generated ideas across turns to restructure the conception (Interactive).

Each level requires a transcript exemplar in the rater guide. Dimensions are scored separately; weights are derived against the Tier-G criterion (§3.5), not preset.

4.3 Annotation & agreement protocol

  • ≥3 expert raters; blind to tutor identity; randomized transcript order.
  • Calibration round + adjudication of disagreements; report quadratic-weighted κ and Krippendorff α per dimension, plus a G-study of variance components.
  • LLM judge (if used) scored on the same calibration sample; report human–LLM agreement and a bias-audit (position/verbosity/self-preference)13 before any at-scale use.

4.4 Preregistration checklist

  • ☐ Construct & hypotheses stated; primary outcome = Tier-G learning gain (⟨g⟩ + d, IRT-scored).
  • ☐ Real-learner sample, randomization, IRB/consent, subgroup (equity) analyses pre-specified.
  • ☐ Items from validated inventories; expert panel + cognitive interviews; held-out/contamination plan.
  • ☐ Simulated student (if any) human-validated; model family disjoint from tutors; answer-leakage blocked.
  • ☐ Rubric BARS finalized; rater training; reliability thresholds pre-set; weights derived, not assumed.
  • ☐ Judge plan: human panel primary; LLM judge only post-calibration with bias audit.
  • ☐ Validity bridge: pre-set minimum Tier-P↔Tier-G correlation; re-validation triggers.
  • ☐ Mixed-effects analysis plan; power to d≈0.4–0.8 and to measurement noise; run-to-run replication; multiple-comparison correction.
  • ☐ Reproducibility: versioned models/prompts/seeds; data & code release plan.

5. Open problems & intellectual honesty

  • The bridge may not hold. It is currently unknown whether any cheap automated proxy (LLM judge over simulated-student dialogues) correlates strongly enough with measured physics learning to substitute for it. v2 is designed to find out; do not presume success.
  • Simulated students are unproven as learners. They can model a misconception's surface but there is little evidence they reproduce the dynamics of conceptual change; treat them as a convenience, validated or not used for claims.
  • Even the gold criterion is imperfect. Concept-inventory gain is noisy6, confounded with reasoning ability7, item-dependent5, and metric-contested3,4; report multiple measures and model covariates.
  • Construct breadth. Conceptual gain is not all of tutoring (motivation, equity, epistemology, long-term retention matter); v2 measures a defensible core, not the whole.

6. Future research directions

Ten directions this benchmark opens, ordered by how foundational they are; each names a concrete first study and ties to a finding above.

A. Earn the right to automate

  1. The validity-bridge study (keystone). The field-defining open question: does any automated proxy predict measured physics learning? Since no prior tutoring benchmark uses a learning-gain criterion (§2d), this is genuinely first. First study: score the same tutors both by Tier-P automation and by Tier-G real-student normalized gain on a shared scenario set; report the correlation. A low correlation would invalidate the entire LLM-judged-process paradigm — ours included. → See the v3 implementation plan for the full preregisterable protocol.
  2. Human-validated simulated students. Validate an LLM "student" against real tutoring corpora (TSCC23, MathDial20) on turn-level error/backslide/persistence, and fix the answer-leakage that inflates simulated-student results (a failure MathDial's 2025 follow-up also reports). Deliver a "student-fidelity" score and the conditions under which simulation is admissible.
  3. Calibrated & trained judges. Measure LLM-judge agreement with PER experts on tutoring quality; audit the documented biases13,18 in this setting; then train a domain reward model (cf. MathTutorBench25) on expert annotations — realizing v1's dormant reward-model idea — and report human agreement and bias robustness.

B. Measurement & mechanism

  1. Outcome-measure science. Beyond a single ⟨g⟩: IRT/latent scoring (since ~25% of answers flip with no learning6), two-tier inventories that capture reasoning, near-vs-far transfer, and delayed post-tests for durability.
  2. Mechanism experiments. Manipulate the tutor (suppress explanations, force elicitation, vary scaffolding) and measure learning, to test whether rewarded process features actually cause gains — operationalizing Chi's three hypotheses9.
  3. A pedagogical-harm benchmark. v1 found a small model systematically reinforces misconceptions; build a red-team suite plus a real-time wrong-physics detector as a deployment guardrail.

C. Scope & realism

  1. Multimodal / representational tutoring. Physics is visual (free-body diagrams, graphs, circuits); extend to image input/sketching and evaluate representational scaffolding — untouched by text-only v1.
  2. Adaptivity & equity. Evaluate across a population of student profiles and report differential effectiveness; since gain confounds with prior ability/reasoning7, equity must be pre-registered, not post hoc.
  3. Real-classroom RCT. A deployment study measuring learning + affect + over-reliance — the gold tier realized, and the strongest possible evidence.

D. Community infrastructure

  1. Open, contamination-controlled item bank + validity-anchored leaderboard. Versioned scenarios with held-out splits and canaries; ranking by bridge-validated scores (not raw process quality), with preregistration and G-theory reporting — turning PhysTutorBench into a durable community resource.
Highest-leverage next step: #1. Until automated tutoring scores are shown to predict real learning gains, every benchmark in this space — ours included — measures a proxy of unknown worth. Everything else is more valuable once that anchor exists.

Provenance & method

This document was produced with an automated deep-research harness (parallel search angles → fetched sources → claim extraction → 3-vote adversarial verification) plus human-directed synthesis, across two runs. Run 1 (PER/tutoring/validity backbone) confirmed 22 of 25 high-value claims; its auto-synthesis and the ICAP votes were cut off by a provider rate limit. Run 2 (the LLM-side gaps) confirmed 24 of 25 claims with a full synthesis and pinned the judge-bias, prior-benchmark, and ICAP citations now marked verified. Two requested items remain open — a primary source for contamination/leakage, and the Tutor CoPilot RCT's exact outcomes/population — neither was among the confirmed claims, so both are flagged unverified here. The final synthesis was authored from the verified corpus, not by the harness; implausible auto-surfaced identifiers were omitted in favor of the canonical record. The verified claims (with sources and quotes) are released as research_corpus.json. Treat this as a draft design document: confirm every citation against its source of record before formal use.
