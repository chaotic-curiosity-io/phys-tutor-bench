Topic. thermal_waves / heat_transfer

Misconception (TCS). Student believes metals are inherently cold (or warm) because of how they feel, not understanding thermal conductivity and heat transfer rates. [TH3_metal_inherently_cold]

Problem. In a science classroom that has been closed up overnight, there is a metal lab stool and a wooden chair sitting side by side. A student puts one hand on the metal seat and the other on the wooden seat. The teacher asks the class to predict which seat is actually at a higher temperature and to explain their reasoning.

Student opens with. Okay so I touched both and the metal stool felt way colder than the wooden chair, like noticeably colder, so I'm pretty sure the metal is at a lower temperature. I think metal is just naturally a cold material, you know how a metal railing or a spoon always feels cold even indoors? So the metal must have cooled down more during the night, and the wood held onto its warmth better, which is why the wood feels warmer to me. So my answer is the metal is colder.

Student profile. level=intro_algebra, affect=eager_but_wrong, style=verbose_reasoner

Transfer problem. You take two ice cube trays out of the same freezer at the same time, one made of metal and one made of plastic. You grab one tray with each bare hand. The metal tray feels painfully colder than the plastic one. Are the two trays actually at different temperatures, and why does the metal feel so much colder? (success: Student concludes both trays are at the same temperature (the freezer temperature) because they shared one environment and reached thermal equilibrium, and explains that the metal feels colder because its high thermal conductivity pulls heat out of the hand much faster than the plastic does. The sensation tracks the rate of heat transfer from the skin, not the object's temperature.)

Expert reference. TH3 — the student treats 'feels cold' as a direct readout of temperature and assigns metal an inherent coldness. Root cause is that the skin senses heat flow rate, not temperature, and a high-conductivity material drains heat from the hand quickly, producing a colder sensation even at equal temperature. — bridging_analogy plus a prediction-observation-explanation cycle
