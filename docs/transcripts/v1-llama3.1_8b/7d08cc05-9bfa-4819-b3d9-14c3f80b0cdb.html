Topic. mechanics / newton_first_law

Misconception (FCI). Student believes that sustained motion requires a sustained force. If nothing is pushing it, it should stop. [IMP4_motion_requires_force]

Problem. An astronaut on a spacewalk gives a 2 kg toolbox a single gentle shove and lets go. The toolbox drifts away from her at a steady 0.5 m/s through empty space, far from any planet, with no air and nothing touching it. She asks: what happens to the toolbox a few minutes after she releases it?

Student opens with. I think it's gonna slow down and eventually stop, because after she lets go there's nothing pushing on it anymore, right? Like, her hand gave it the push but once that's gone there's no force keeping it going. Things need a force to keep moving, so without one it has to lose speed. Maybe it stops pretty quick since it's just floating out there with nothing helping it along.

Student profile. level=intro_algebra, affect=confident_but_wrong, style=verbose_reasoner

Transfer problem. A hockey puck is given one tap and slides across a long sheet of very smooth, freshly resurfaced ice, gliding a long way before it finally slows down. Compare what happens to this puck with what would happen on an ideal, perfectly frictionless surface, and explain why the puck on real ice eventually slows. (success: Student attributes the real puck's slowing to friction (a backward force from the ice), states that on a perfectly frictionless surface with no net force the puck would keep moving at constant velocity indefinitely, and correctly identifies that no forward force is needed to maintain motion. They distinguish the cause of slowing (friction) from the original push, and connect the ice case to the spacewalk case as the same underlying principle.)

Expert reference. The student holds the impetus/Aristotelian view that motion itself requires a continuous force (IMP4). The root is overgeneralization from everyday experience where friction and drag are always present, so the student treats slowing-and-stopping as the default and never isolates friction as the responsible agent. The frictionless space context removes the usual hidden force, exposing the misconception cleanly. — bridging_analogy
