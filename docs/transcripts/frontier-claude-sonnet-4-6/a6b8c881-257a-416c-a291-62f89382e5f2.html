Topic. modern_quantum / quantum_states

Misconception (QMVI). Student interprets quantum superposition as a classical statistical mixture — the system is definitely in one state, we just don't know which. [QM9_superposition_classical_mixture]

Problem. A single electron passes through a double-slit setup one particle at a time. Before hitting the detection screen, with no which-path detector present, the electron is described by a superposition of 'went through slit A' and 'went through slit B.' Over many electrons an interference pattern of bright and dark fringes builds up. A student is asked what the superposition means for an individual electron in flight, and why dark fringes appear.

Student opens with. I guess the superposition just means the electron really did go through one slit or the other, A or B, and we just don't know which one until it lands. So it's kind of like a coin we haven't looked at yet, definitely heads or tails. The bright spots are probably... where more electrons happen to land? I'm not totally sure how the dark stripes work if each electron picks a real slit, but maybe the dark spots are just places where, um, fewer of them end up by chance. I might be off on that part.

Student profile. level=intro_calculus, affect=confident_but_wrong, style=hedging_guesser

Transfer problem. A Mach-Zehnder interferometer sends a single photon into a device with two paths, path 1 and path 2, that recombine at a final beam splitter feeding two detectors, D-bright and D-dark. With both paths open and balanced, every single photon is detected at D-bright and never at D-dark. Now you place a tiny non-absorbing marker that records which path each photon took (without blocking it). Predict what happens at D-dark in each case, and explain why a picture in which each photon 'definitely took path 1 or path 2, we just don't know which' fails to account for the both-paths-open result. (success: Student predicts that with both paths open and no which-path marker, the photon always lands at D-bright and never at D-dark due to destructive interference of the two path amplitudes, whereas adding a which-path marker destroys the interference and makes D-dark start clicking (roughly 50/50). They explain that a classical 'definitely path 1 or path 2' mixture would send some photons to D-dark even with both paths open, so the complete darkness of D-dark proves the amplitudes for both paths coexist and interfere for each single photon. They conclude superposition is not ignorance of a definite path but the coherent presence of both, with interference as the empirical signature.)

Expert reference. The student treats superposition as a classical statistical mixture (QM9): each electron 'really' went through one slit and the superposition merely encodes our ignorance. The root is that a mixture and a superposition both produce probabilistic single outcomes, so without interference the two are indistinguishable; the misconception survives because the student has not connected dark fringes to the cancellation of amplitudes, which a classical mixture (adding probabilities, never negative) cannot produce. — socratic_questioning
