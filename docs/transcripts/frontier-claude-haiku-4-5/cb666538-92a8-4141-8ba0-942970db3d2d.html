Misconception (FCI). Student believes that heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones in all situations, not just in the presence of air resistance. [GRAV1_heavier_falls_faster]

Problem. A demonstrator climbs onto a balcony and, at exactly the same instant, drops a heavy 5 kg dumbbell and a light 0.2 kg apple from the same height of 6 meters. Air resistance is negligible for both. Question: which one hits the ground first, or do they land at the same time?

Student profile. level=intro_algebra, affect=eager_but_wrong, style=question_asker

Transfer problem. On the airless Moon, an astronaut holds a steel hammer and a bird feather at the same height and releases them simultaneously. Predict whether the hammer or the feather reaches the lunar surface first, and explain your reasoning. Then state how your answer would differ on Earth and why. (success: Student predicts the hammer and feather land at the same time on the Moon because there is no air resistance and gravitational acceleration is the same for all masses. They justify it by noting the heavier object has both more gravitational force and proportionally more inertia, so acceleration (a = F/m = g) is independent of mass. They correctly attribute any difference on Earth solely to air resistance acting on the feather, not to the feather being lighter in a fundamental sense.)

Expert reference. The student believes weight directly sets fall speed (GRAV1), reasoning that more gravitational force must mean faster falling. The root is neglecting that mass appears twice — as the source of greater gravitational force AND as greater inertia resisting acceleration — so the two effects cancel and acceleration is mass-independent. The student's confusion is productive: they are already sensing the competing effect ('does lighter mean gravity pulls it less?'). — socratic_questioning
